More SAE latents can refine an old concept

Does scaling a sparse autoencoder discover new concepts, or allocate ever more latents to familiar curved ones? This paper models SAE training as a capacity-allocation problem. Its key warning is conditional: reconstruction loss can keep improving while the number of distinct underlying features grows much more slowly than dictionary width.

Preparation: orthogonal subspaces, power laws, SAEs and manifolds. Related: capture regimes.

Reduce optimization to an allocation problem

Let underlying feature $i$ occur with probability $p_i$. It may be multidimensional, occupying subspace $S_i$. Let $n_i$ be the number of SAE latents devoted to it and $L_i(n_i)$ the corresponding per-feature reconstruction-plus-sparsity cost. Under assumptions that subspaces are orthogonal and each latent belongs to one feature, expected loss becomes $\sum_i p_iL_i(n_i)$ with $\sum_i n_i=N$, the dictionary budget.

Those assumptions are substantial. Real features can correlate, overlap, or absorb one another. The model does not derive arbitrary SAE training dynamics; it analyzes an idealized optimum once costs are specified. That restriction makes the mechanism transparent: every additional latent is spent where it offers the greatest weighted reduction in loss.

For discrete unit features, the first latent reduces per-feature cost from 1 to a sparsity cost $\lambda$, and later latents add no benefit. The optimum gives one latent to each of the most frequent features. If $p_i\propto i^{-(1+\alpha)}$, the unrepresented tail behaves like $\int_N^\infty i^{-(1+\alpha)}di\propto N^{-\alpha}$ for $\alpha\gt0$. A loss power law can therefore reflect a frequency tail.

When geometry competes with discovery

For a curved feature, suppose $L_i(n_i)=c_in_i^{-\beta}$ over a relevant range, with $c_i\gt0$ and $\beta\gt0$. Relax integer counts to positive real numbers and introduce multiplier $\mu$ for the total budget. Differentiating $\sum_i p_ic_in_i^{-\beta}+\mu(\sum_i n_i-N)$ gives

$$-\beta p_ic_in_i^{-(\beta+1)}+\mu=0,\qquad n_i=\left(\frac{\beta p_ic_i}{\mu}\right)^{1/(\beta+1)}.$$

Thus common difficult manifolds receive many latents. The integer threshold for discovering a new feature matters and is omitted from this smooth relaxation. The paper's fuller analysis finds distinct regimes: when $\alpha\lt\beta$, discovered-feature count grows proportionally to $N$; when $\beta\lt\alpha$, it grows as $N^{(1+\beta)/(1+\alpha)}$, so the fraction of latents corresponding to newly discovered features tends to zero. The boundary case requires separate analysis.

The loss can then scale with a geometric approximation exponent rather than the frequency-tail exponent. Observing a clean scaling curve alone cannot tell which mechanism is responsible.

Original worked example: the next latent

Suppose a common manifold appears with probability $0.8$ and its cost is $L(n)=1/n$. With four assigned latents, a fifth reduces weighted loss by $0.8(1/4-1/5)=0.04$. A previously unseen discrete feature occurring with probability $0.03$ would reduce loss by only $0.03$ if reconstructed without a sparsity charge. The optimal next allocation refines the common manifold.

At five manifold latents, a sixth reduces loss by $0.8(1/5-1/6)=0.8/30\approx0.0267$, now less than $0.03$. The rare feature wins that comparison. This is a teaching calculation, not a reported trained SAE. It shows that delayed discovery need not mean permanent exclusion, and that marginal rather than total error determines the next allocation.

A framed concern, not a settled diagnosis

The paper trains SAEs on synthetic geometric distributions and inspects decoder-neighbor similarities in real SAEs. It explicitly does not resolve whether real SAEs occupy the pathological regime. The unknowns include the true frequency distribution and the relevant per-feature approximation curves. Some synthetic radial variations lead to early plateaus rather than sustained tiling, weakening the concern under those geometries.

High similarity among decoder atoms can indicate tiling but is not conclusive: hierarchical features, correlated concepts, or duplicate solutions can also produce it. Low loss and readable labels do not measure feature coverage. A useful evaluation must separately track known factors, geometric refinement, reconstruction, sparsity, and intervention fidelity.

Exercises

  1. Conceptual: Why can improved reconstruction coexist with poor growth in discovered-feature count?
  2. Mathematical: Under the smooth allocation model with equal $c_i$ and $\beta=1$, what is the ratio of allocations for frequencies $0.36$ and $0.04$?
  3. Critical experiment: Design a synthetic test separating more precise manifold tiling from discovery of rare factors.
