Why more capacity can preserve rare learning

Can a small model fail to learn a task that it can represent perfectly in isolation? This paper argues yes: a mixed training distribution creates competition for shared resources. Larger models can fit common tasks sufficiently well that their gradients weaken, allowing rare-task updates to survive until the next relevant example.

Preparation: eigenvalues and projectors, gradients, mixture distributions, controlled experiments.

Expressible is not the same as learned

The paper first uses a phenomenological scaling law to distinguish loss reductions accessible through more data from those requiring more model capacity under a fixed training setup. Extrapolating a power law to infinite data is an assumption, explicitly qualified by the authors. Aggregate loss differences also do not identify which semantic task is missing. The controlled regression setting supplies the more precise account.

Each task $k$ has frequency $\pi_k\gt0$, with $\sum_k\pi_k=1$, and a positive semidefinite covariance $C_k$. Its eigenvalues $\lambda_{k,j}$ measure how much predictive signal lies in feature direction $j$. Different tasks occupy orthogonal subspaces in the theory. A shared encoder $U\in\mathbb R^{d\times N}$ has orthonormal columns, so $P_U=UU^\top$ projects onto an $N$-dimensional retained space. Task-specific decoders read that representation.

A frequency-weighted ranking of features

After optimizing the decoders, the mixture loss is $L_N(U)=\sum_k\pi_k\operatorname{tr}((I-P_U)C_k)$. Define $M=\sum_k\pi_kC_k$. Linearity and cyclic invariance of trace give

$$L_N(U)=\operatorname{tr}(M)-\operatorname{tr}(UU^\top M)=\operatorname{tr}(M)-\operatorname{tr}(U^\top MU).$$

Minimizing loss therefore maximizes captured variance. The variational characterization of eigenvalues says the optimum retains the top $N$ eigenvectors of $M$. Under the orthogonal-task assumption, their eigenvalues are exactly $\pi_k\lambda_{k,j}$, the paper's feature utilities. A rare high-quality feature can lose to a weaker but more frequent one. Increasing training time cannot change this global optimum unless the objective, distribution, or capacity changes.

The dynamic argument asks whether a rare update persists. For common tasks $F$, let $M_F=\sum_{k\in F}\pi_k C_k$, let $R=I-P_U$ be the projector onto directions outside the current representation, and define residual signal $\delta_F=\operatorname{tr}(RM_F)$. Under the constraint $U^\top U=I$, the common-task contribution to gradient-flow velocity is $G_F=2RM_FU$. This is a projected field; it is not the unconstrained Euclidean loss gradient $-2M_FU$.

Write $\|A\|_F^2=\operatorname{tr}(A^\top A)$ for squared Frobenius norm, and let $\lambda_1(M_F)$ be the largest eigenvalue. Since $U$ has orthonormal columns and $R$ is a projector,

$$\frac14\|G_F\|_F^2=\|RM_FU\|_F^2\leq\|RM_F\|_F^2=\operatorname{tr}(RM_F^2)\leq\lambda_1(M_F)\operatorname{tr}(RM_F)=\lambda_1(M_F)\delta_F.$$

The last inequality uses $M_F^2\preceq\lambda_1(M_F)M_F$, which follows by comparing nonnegative eigenvalues, and positivity of trace against $R\succeq0$. Taking square roots gives the paper's bound $\|G_F\|_F\leq2\sqrt{\lambda_1(M_F)\delta_F}$. Fitting common-task signal reduces this source of parameter motion in the constrained regression model. It does not prove that all transformer gradients become orthogonal at large scale.

Original worked example: capacity allocation

Take common-task frequency $0.9$ and eigenvalues $(1,0.2)$, and rare-task frequency $0.1$ with one eigenvalue $1$. Utilities are $0.9$, $0.18$, and $0.1$. A width-two encoder retains both common directions and none of the rare direction. Its rare-task loss is $1$ despite being able to solve the rare task with a width-one encoder when trained alone. Width three retains all directions. This is an analytic teaching construction, not a reproduction of the paper's training runs.

Changing the data mixture also changes the result. At rare frequency $0.3$, the utilities become $0.7$, $0.14$, and $0.3$. Width two now retains the dominant common direction and the rare direction. The trade-off is common-task residual loss $0.2$. Capacity and data balance are alternative control variables, but they optimize different weighted objectives.

A simple retention analogy makes the temporal issue visible. If each common batch multiplies a stored rare signal by $r\in(0,1)$, a gap of $G$ batches retains fraction $r^G$. With $r=0.99$, a hundred intervening batches retain about $0.366$. This illustrative recurrence is not the paper's full encoder dynamics; it shows why matching long-run frequency alone need not match learning.

Evidence and its reach

The paper verifies utility ordering in synthetic mixtures, varies injection gaps while preserving long-run rare-task frequency, and extends the investigation to OLMo pretraining with controlled injected tasks. It examines behavior, representations, and gradient interference. Those complementary measurements strengthen the proposed explanation beyond an aggregate scaling curve.

The theory assumes orthogonal blocks and optimized task-specific decoders. Real linguistic tasks overlap, task frequency is hard to define, and optimization may find different solutions. Controlled novel tasks reduce frequency ambiguity but need not behave like every naturally occurring skill. A task that is rare, valuable, and computationally difficult may respond differently to repetition, curriculum, or architectural specialization. The paper identifies a mechanism and useful design variables rather than establishing that parameter scaling is the only route to better rare-task learning.

Exercises

  1. Conceptual: Why does failure in a mixture not establish an expressivity limitation?
  2. Mathematical: In the worked example, find the rare-task frequency at which its feature overtakes the second common feature.
  3. Critical experiment: How can you vary rare-example spacing without confounding it with total frequency?
