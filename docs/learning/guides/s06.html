Control what training generalizes

A learner can extract the correct lesson and several unwanted lessons from the same example. Training on a correct mathematical solution in pirate speech may improve calculation, encourage pirate phrasing, increase verbosity, and reinforce incidental transitions. Filtering whole examples forces a crude decision: accept or reject their bundled lessons. Thomas McGrath's February 5, 2026 essay asks whether interpretation of the learner's internals can support a finer decision about what to learn.

This is an agenda essay, with illustrative mechanisms and references to early experiments. Its north star is intentional design: connect semantic feedback to model components and use that connection to guide training. It is not a complete algorithm for translating arbitrary natural-language instructions into reliable weight updates. Optional preparation: gradients and learning, causal reasoning, and the later feature-reward demonstration.

Observation, intervention, and a feedback loop

The essay separates understanding a system from controlling its development, then argues that the former can enable the latter. An interpretation tool supplies semantic measurements of what a datum affects. A design algorithm modifies the learning process using those measurements and a specification. New measurements check whether training is moving toward the specification. This is a feedback-control proposal; it requires a sensor, an actuator, and a target that are all useful at the relevant timescale.

Interpretability is therefore more than a descriptive dashboard in this proposal. A dashboard that reports “pirate style is increasing” while offering no effective way to alter learning does not close the loop. Conversely, a successful training intervention does not automatically validate the semantic interpretation used to motivate it: some other correlated property may explain its success. The scientific task includes evaluating the sensor, actuator, and behavioral outcome separately.

The essay's first broad application is richer supervision. Natural language can specify which aspect of an answer is wrong, whereas a single preference bit ranks the whole answer. Its second application is alignment of the training process: a constitution or another evaluator could select intended generalizations as learning happens. Both depend on identifying semantically useful components. They remain more ambitious than merely adding a natural-language critique to the next prompt.

A local mathematical translation

The following control formulation is original teaching material. Let $\theta_t\in\mathbb R^P$ be model parameters after training step $t$, $d_t$ the next data batch, and $g_t=\nabla_\theta\ell(\theta_t;d_t)$ its ordinary loss gradient. Let $b(\theta)\in\mathbb R^K$ collect $K$ behavioral measurements on a fixed distribution, and $b^*$ be the desired vector. A controlled update has the form

$$\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta_t u_t,\qquad u_t=F(g_t,I_t,b^*-b(\theta_t)),$$

where $\eta_t\gt0$ is a step size, $I_t$ is the current interpretation, and $F$ is a design rule. This notation identifies the missing technology rather than supplying it. A specification in language must somehow choose measurable targets and permissible tradeoffs; the interpretation must link those targets to learning; and $F$ must produce useful updates under an actual optimizer.

For a small update $\delta\theta$, define the behavioral Jacobian $J\in\mathbb R^{K\times P}$ by $J_{kj}=\partial b_k/\partial\theta_j$. The first-order response is $\delta b\approx J\delta\theta$. Even an ideal local controller cannot independently control arbitrary behavior vectors if $J$ lacks the required rank. With a norm bound $\|\delta\theta\|\le r$, small singular values of $J$ make some behavioral changes expensive. Semantic separability and numerical controllability are related questions, but they are not identical.

The essay uses sparse-autoencoder (SAE) features to dissect a gradient signal in activation space. For token position $t$, let $h_t\in\mathbb R^d$ be the residual activation, $d_k\in\mathbb R^d$ decoder direction $k$, $z_{tk}$ its feature strength, and $q_t=\partial\ell/\partial h_t$ the loss gradient. A contribution score of the form $z_{tk}d_k^\top q_t$, averaged over positions, estimates first-order sensitivity to that feature's contribution. A large score can connect a semantic feature to the current training signal. It does not decompose the entire parameter update into independent semantic mechanisms.

In particular, $d_k$ lives in activation space, while $g_t$ lives in parameter space. Projecting a parameter gradient “away from an SAE direction” without a mapping between those spaces is a type error. Even after defining such a mapping, eliminating one local direction need not eliminate the behavior: another layer or direction can implement it, and representation geometry can drift during training.

Original worked example: when local control is costly

Let parameters $(\theta_1,\theta_2)$ control a toy math score $b_M=\theta_1+\theta_2$ and a toy pirate score $b_P=\theta_2$. Assume both scores are dimensionless and these relationships are exact. An ordinary proposed update $\delta\theta=(0.1,0.1)$ changes scores by $(0.2,0.1)$. We want the same math improvement with no pirate increase. Here

$$J=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad J\delta\theta_{\rm desired}=\begin{pmatrix}0.2\\0\end{pmatrix}.$$

The second equation forces $\delta\theta_2=0$, and the first gives $\delta\theta_1=0.2$. Thus selective learning is locally possible, but the required update norm is $0.2$, compared with $\sqrt{0.02}\approx0.1414$ for the original update. If we simply remove the second parameter's update, math improves only by $0.1$. “Remove unwanted learning” and “preserve all useful learning” are distinct requirements.

Now change the represented scores to $b_M=\theta_1+\theta_2$ and $b_P=\theta_1+\theta_2$. The rows of $J$ coincide, and no parameter update can change one score while holding the other fixed. The bottleneck is the representation assumed by the toy model. A larger network might learn new circuitry that separates the tasks; a local controller cannot assume that possibility is already available in its current coordinate system.

This calculation captures one reason the source refuses to identify intentional design with naive gradient surgery. In a fixed toy coordinate system, deleting a projection is easy. In a trainable neural network, the algorithm and representation can co-adapt, so the behavior may reappear through new coordinates. That observation motivates better algorithms; it is not a theorem that all gradient modifications fail.

What “not fighting backprop” can mean operationally

The essay warns that directly optimizing against a probe on a student's changing activations can teach the student to fool the probe. A useful alternative is to run an evaluator on a frozen reference model's activations for the student's generated tokens, then optimize a conventional reinforcement-learning objective using that score. There is no differentiable path from the student's parameters through the frozen feature extractor. The student cannot cheaply reduce the score merely by rotating its own representation coordinates.

For generated response $y$, frozen evaluator reward $R_0(x,y)$, and policy $\pi_\theta$, the score-function gradient is $\nabla_\theta\mathbb E[R_0]=\mathbb E[R_0\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)]$, with a suitable baseline often subtracted for variance reduction. The reward still shapes which tokens are selected. It can therefore be exploited at the output level: an evaluator blind spot can reward evasive wording, omissions, or another undesirable behavior. Freezing removes one adaptation channel; it does not solve reward misspecification.

The later RLFR article supplies an empirical case for this architecture. It should be read with its decomposition of policy, in-context, and direct intervention effects. This essay's preliminary optimism about frozen probes is not an independent replication of those results. Likewise, its mention of stochastic parameter decomposition is a research direction for interpreting weights and gradients; the later VPD method is a distinct development, explained in the parameter companion.

The hard problem: semantics change with learning

A feedback controller assumes its measurements retain their meaning. If a feature labeled “caution” at the start of training later responds to politeness instead, the controller may optimize a moving proxy while believing it is enforcing caution. Monitoring only the feature's numerical activation will miss this drift. Repeated semantic checks on fresh data, intervention tests, and comparisons with independent behavioral labels are needed.

The same issue appears in long rollouts. A feature measured at a token is not automatically a property of the whole trajectory. Sampling is discrete, paths branch, earlier interventions alter future inputs, and small policy changes can create states absent from the evaluator's training data. Cheap token-level feedback and expensive trajectory-level verification address different objects. The essay identifies long-horizon interpretation and changing component semantics as unresolved technical challenges.

Evidence audit and serious counterarguments

The source presents the pirate example as a toy illustration and explicitly says simpler data methods could address it. That admission prevents a common mistake: demonstrating that semantic control is possible somewhere does not establish that an elaborate interpreter is the best way to achieve the effect. A meaningful comparison includes prompting, matched counterexamples, ordinary fine-tuning, and preference training with the same data and compute.

The strongest skeptical objection is that the tool used to train a model cannot also be an independent test of its safety. The essay proposes using different interpretation methods for training and verification, potentially spending more compute at verification. Diversity helps only to the extent that errors differ. Two tools built on the same sparse-linear assumption or shared labels can have correlated blind spots. Separate implementations are weaker than separate evidence.

Other alternatives remain live. Better dataset curation might capture most practical benefit without mechanistic decomposition. Behavioral control may be achievable without understanding internal algorithms. Rich semantics might be unstable or too expensive to maintain. Conversely, useful partial understanding may support interventions long before complete reverse engineering. The essay argues for exploring that middle ground; it does not settle these alternatives.

Reading locators and explanatory target

Exercises

  1. Concept. Why does running a reward probe on a frozen model help, and what reward-hacking route remains?
  2. Math. In the first toy model, constrain $\|\delta\theta\|_2\le0.15$ and require no pirate change. What is the largest possible math improvement? Compare it with the ordinary update.
  3. Critique. Design a training/test interpretation split that provides stronger evidence than applying two different SAE models to the same labels.
