Why a brittle memory can occupy a flat dataset direction

The apparent paradox is the lesson: an individual memorized example can have sharp loss curvature, while a direction supporting only a few examples can have low curvature after averaging over the dataset. The source uses approximate curvature to edit model weights and suppress broad memorization, while investigating which other capabilities the edit damages.

Preparation: eigendecompositions, gradients and Hessians, expectations. Related: rare-task learning.

Keep the averaging operation visible

Let $\theta\in\mathbb R^p$ denote model parameters and $\ell_i(\theta)$ the loss of example $i$. For direction $v\in\mathbb R^p$ of unit norm, a second-order expansion gives

$$\ell_i(\theta+\epsilon v)\approx\ell_i(\theta)+\epsilon\nabla\ell_i^\top v+\frac{\epsilon^2}{2}v^\top H_i v,$$

where $H_i=\nabla^2\ell_i$ is the example Hessian. The dataset loss $L=N^{-1}\sum_i\ell_i$ has Hessian $H=N^{-1}\sum_iH_i$, so $v^\top Hv=N^{-1}\sum_i v^\top H_iv$. A direction can be sharply important for one example and weak on average. Curvature also depends on parameterization and the evaluation distribution; it is not a coordinate-free measure of memory.

The source's intuition is that shared computations affect many examples, while isolated memorization affects relatively few. This is a statistical signature, not a categorical definition: rare useful facts or specialized computations can also appear in low-average-curvature directions. That ambiguity is central to the observed capability trade-offs.

From statistics to a weight edit

The explainer describes four steps: collect activation and gradient statistics on unlabeled training text, infer approximate curvature through K-FAC, express weight matrices in the resulting bases, and remove selected low-curvature components. K-FAC approximates a matrix-block curvature using a Kronecker product of activation and output-gradient second moments. This avoids constructing an enormous full matrix. The gradient-second-moment matrix used below is positive semidefinite and is often treated as a Fisher-type curvature proxy. It is not generally the exact loss Hessian from the Taylor expansion: an exact Hessian can have negative eigenvalues. Equality needs additional model, loss, and expectation assumptions. Consequently, the motivating Hessian identity explains an intuition, while the practical K-FAC edit uses an approximate sensitivity measure.

For a linear layer $y=Wx$, the gradient has outer-product form $\nabla_W\ell=gx^\top$, where $g$ is the loss gradient with respect to $y$. With column-wise vectorization, $\operatorname{vec}(gx^\top)=x\otimes g$. Its gradient second-moment matrix is $F_{\rm emp}=\mathbb E[(xx^\top)\otimes(gg^\top)]$. A K-FAC-style factorization approximates this as $A\otimes G$, where $A=\mathbb E[xx^\top]$ and $G=\mathbb E[gg^\top]$. This replaces a joint expectation by a product, so dependence between the factors can matter. The empirical gradient second moment is positive semidefinite and is not generally the exact loss Hessian, which can be indefinite. Fisher, generalized-Gauss–Newton, and Hessian curvature coincide only under additional likelihood, expectation, or local-model assumptions. The factorization is therefore an efficient curvature proxy, not an exact identity derived from the Hessian expansion. Eigenvectors of $A$ and $G$ define its matrix coordinates, and its eigenvalues are products of factor eigenvalues.

This is a general K-FAC explanation, not a full specification of the paper's edit thresholds. The source notes that known memorized examples are used to choose hyperparameters. “Optimization-free” therefore does not mean free of data selection or tuning.

Original worked example: sharp but rare

Suppose there are 1000 examples. A memory coordinate $v_m$ has individual curvature 100 on one example and zero on all others. Its dataset curvature is $0.1$. A shared computation coordinate $v_c$ has curvature 1 on every example, so its dataset curvature is 1. The memory is individually sharper yet ten times flatter in the average objective. This is an original exact calculation, not an estimate from the source models.

If an edit removes all directions below curvature $0.2$, it removes $v_m$ and preserves $v_c$. But a rare valid medical fact supported by the same pattern would also be removed. The threshold knows frequency-weighted sensitivity, not whether the content is desirable. Reducing recitation is therefore different from precise deletion of one specified memory and different again from preserving all general knowledge.

What the source actually finds

The source compares its edit with BalancedSubnet on known memorized sequences and held-out historical quotes. It reports broader suppression of unseen recitation, investigates vision transformers trained with random-label noise, and examines a spectrum of downstream tasks. Logical reasoning is relatively robust in its tested settings; arithmetic and factual recall can be harmed. The source treats arithmetic mistakes inside otherwise coherent reasoning chains as evidence for that distinction, not proof that all mathematical reasoning survives.

The method's limitations follow from its mechanism: a curvature estimate from finite text may miss rare directions; K-FAC is approximate; edits can be heavy-handed; and capability categories are not cleanly separated. Random-label memorization is a useful controlled case but is not the same as verbatim language recall. A robust evaluation needs task-level results, held-out recitation tests, and meaningful baselines at matched general capability.

Exercises

  1. Conceptual: Why are individual-example sharpness and dataset flatness compatible?
  2. Mathematical: A direction has curvature 40 on fraction $p$ of examples and zero otherwise. When is its average curvature below 0.5?
  3. Critical experiment: Design a test separating removal of unwanted recitation from loss of rare useful knowledge.
