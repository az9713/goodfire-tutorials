Source 37 · Multi-organization research synthesis · August 2025

An attribution graph is an execution trace

What do circuit-tracing tools reveal, and what remains outside their view? This landscape report places attribution graphs among behavioral experiments, probes, sparse decompositions, and interventions. Its recurring distinction is between surfacing a useful prompt-specific hypothesis and recovering a general algorithm.

Preparation: linear maps and derivatives, MLPs and residual streams, causal validation, sparse replacement models. Read the greater-than case study for a concrete example.

Hypothesis generation and testing are complementary

The report treats behavior as indispensable evidence: carefully designed input-output tests can discover phenomena before any internal mechanism is known. Probes test for specified properties; SAEs and transcoders can surface unanticipated structure. Attribution graphs connect active features into candidate computational pathways for one prompt. Follow-up interventions test the proposed pathways.

A graph is not a neutral photograph. It depends on a learned replacement model, a local attribution rule, and a pruning procedure. Per-layer transcoders approximate individual MLP maps; cross-layer transcoders let features write into later layers as well. Different choices trade reconstruction, sparsity, computational cost, and interpretability. The report also recognizes native-neuron analysis as an alternative basis for graphs.

Local contribution versus changed computation

Let scalar output $y$ depend on activation vector $h$. Replacing one component by $h_i+\Delta h_i$ gives first-order prediction $\Delta y\approx(\partial y/\partial h_i)\Delta h_i$. This can rank candidate pathways cheaply. It equals an intervention effect only when higher-order changes are negligible and the intervention does not alter other computational regimes.

A sparse replacement reconstructs $\hat h$ and leaves residual $e=h-\hat h$. A local replacement can preserve the original forward value by adding $e$. But when feature values change, treating the residual as fixed is a modeling choice. Error nodes mark unexplained contributions; they are part of the evidence rather than cosmetic clutter. A graph dominated by them offers limited explanation even if its surviving labels look plausible.

Training sparsity and graph pruning are separate operations. The former changes what the replacement learns; the latter removes parts of an already learned explanation. The report evaluates architecture and pruning trade-offs using replacement scores, and notes that improving output KL divergence need not improve the circuit-oriented metric. Different evaluation quantities answer different questions.

Original worked example: a truthful local trace, a misleading algorithm

Suppose a hidden computation returns $y(a,b)=a+b$ on integers. A replacement uses lookup indicators $z_{ij}=\mathbf1_{a=i,b=j}$ and outputs $\sum_{ij}(i+j)z_{ij}$. For the prompt $(a,b)=(2,3)$, one active node perfectly explains the output 5. The replacement reproduces every covered input-output pair while revealing no shared addition rule.

Alternatively, an internal phase representation could encode numbers on circles and transform them through shared operations. Both descriptions can fit the finite behavior table. This original construction illustrates the report's concern that sparse tools can shatter a smooth geometric computation into lookup-like features. It does not establish which representation a particular model uses.

To discover a global rule, examine a family of prompts, test transformations of operands, and intervene on shared variables. Merely merging many lookup graphs produces a larger table. Generalization and structured causal tests are needed to justify the abstraction “addition.”

What the report reports

The synthesis describes replications of multi-hop reasoning, language-independent pathways, clause-boundary features, spelling properties, and greater-than mechanisms. It explicitly leaves some interpretations unsettled: an apparent collection of rhyme heuristics could reflect genuine distributed heuristics or feature splitting by the transcoder. This is an example of uncertainty that a readable graph alone cannot resolve.

Architecture experiments compare CLTs, per-layer variants, activation functions, skip connections, and weight tying. Their measured trade-offs are model- and setup-specific. The report's future-work agenda includes merging graphs across prompts, understanding suppressive pathways, scaling to long contexts, and testing understanding through downstream auditing tasks. These are open directions, not completed capabilities.

The August 2025 report is a dated field snapshot. Its tool availability, released-model list, and counts should not be read as current platform status. This companion preserves its conceptual and empirical distinctions without relying on live Neuronpedia graphs or video access.

Exercises

  1. Conceptual: Why can a faithful per-prompt replacement still misrepresent global geometry?
  2. Mathematical: Let $y=h^2$ at $h=1$. Compare the linear prediction with the exact change for $\Delta h=1$.
  3. Critical experiment: Design a benchmark of circuit understanding that cannot be passed by attractive graph labels alone.
