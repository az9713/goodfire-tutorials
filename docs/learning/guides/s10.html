Which apparent reasoning forks survive more sampling?

A single reasoning trace hides the many continuations that could have followed each prefix. Forking Paths Analysis estimates the distribution of final answers after resampling those prefixes. Its cost is formidable: noisy low-sample curves can make every token look consequential. This paper asks whether statistical structure—slow variation separated by occasional sharp forks—can recover useful uncertainty dynamics with fewer continuations. The companion covers all thirteen pages of the linked paper; the companion treats the limitations and ablations as part of the result.

Prerequisites: multinomial sampling and inference, counterfactual experiment design, and experimental accounting. Let $t$ denote a position in one fixed, greedily decoded base path. Let $o_t\in\mathbb R^K$ be its distribution over $K$ final-answer classes under the paper's resampling protocol. Here the classes are A, B, C, D, and Other. This is conditional behavioral uncertainty along a selected path, not uncertainty over every possible reasoning trajectory.

The branch-weighted estimator

At a prefix, the method considers alternative next tokens whose model probability exceeds a cutoff. Let $w$ index retained branches, $a_w$ their normalized weights, and $p_{wk}$ the probability of final answer $k$ after choosing branch $w$. Draw $S$ independent continuations per branch and let $C_{wk}$ be their answer counts. Then

$$\hat o_k=\sum_w a_w\frac{C_{wk}}{S},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat o_k)=\frac1S\sum_w a_w^2p_{wk}(1-p_{wk}).$$

The second formula follows because independent branch estimates have zero covariance and a binomial marginal variance $p_{wk}(1-p_{wk})/S$. This is a stratified weighted estimator. It is important not to describe its counts as an ordinary multinomial sample of size $S$. The paper separately explains that drawing a branch according to $a_w$ and then drawing a continuation produces an ordinary categorical sample. Excluding low-probability next tokens also limits the target distribution unless their mass is accounted for.

Original numerical case: why pooling helps and hurts

Consider two branches with weights $0.8,0.2$ and answer-A probabilities $0.9,0.1$. The mixture probability is $0.74$. At $S=25$ samples per branch, the weighted estimator has variance

$$\frac{0.8^2(0.9)(0.1)+0.2^2(0.1)(0.9)}{25}=0.002448,$$

giving standard deviation about $0.0495$. An ordinary 25-sample Bernoulli mixture has variance $0.74(0.26)/25=0.007696$, but uses half as many continuations. This teaching calculation illustrates why both sampling design and token cost matter. Neither is a reported experiment from the paper.

If $m$ neighboring positions truly share the same answer probability and provide independent, equally informative estimates, averaging divides variance by $m$. But if one position has probability $0.9$ and its neighbor $0.1$, averaging estimates $0.5$: variance falls while bias becomes $0.4$ at each side. Smoothing across a genuine fork can therefore make the curve confidently wrong. Change-point segmentation attempts to preserve these boundaries before pooling.

The paper's full estimator combines PELT segmentation, Gaussian-kernel-weighted Dirichlet pooling within segments, and cross-validation on held-out continuation samples.

To make pooling explicit, let $C_{jk}$ be outcome-$k$ counts at observed position $j$, $n_j=\sum_k C_{jk}$, and $\omega_{tj}=\exp[-(t-j)^2/(2h^2)]$ for positions in the same detected segment as $t$, with bandwidth $h\gt0$. Give class $k$ a positive prior pseudocount $\alpha_k$. A kernel-weighted Dirichlet mean is

$$\hat p_{tk}=\frac{\alpha_k+\sum_j\omega_{tj}C_{jk}}{\sum_k\alpha_k+\sum_j\omega_{tj}n_j}.$$

Summing the numerators over $k$ gives the denominator, so the estimated probabilities sum to one. This is a teaching formulation of the pooling principle, not a claim about the paper's selected pseudocounts. Fractional weighted counts are a smoothing rule, so nominal posterior intervals need empirical coverage checks. The $C_{jk}$ here represent categorical continuation counts, not the weighted branch fractions above. Segmentation limits the sum to avoid averaging across a fork. PELT chooses boundaries by minimizing summed within-segment fitting cost plus a penalty per boundary; the penalty trades noisy oversegmentation against missed forks. Cross-validation chooses the penalty and bandwidth using held-out continuation counts.

Total variation distance is $\operatorname{TVD}(p,q)=\frac12\sum_k|p_k-q_k|$. It measures discrepancy between distributions, not answer correctness. A model may be very certain of an incorrect answer.

What the full paper establishes

PDF pages 1–4 describe two 8B-scale models and tinyMMLU, with approximately 1.77 billion generated tokens in the evaluation collection. The reasoning-distilled model is sampled at sentence boundaries, whereas the instruction-tuned model is sampled at token boundaries. Consequently, an interval of four has different semantic and computational meaning across models. Section 3.3 finds noise scaling close to $S^{-1/2}$, consistent with independent sampling. That scaling supports a noise model; it does not prove that all residual changes are mechanistically meaningful forks.

Appendix A, PDF page 6, is essential. The reference curves are themselves finite-sample estimates. The nominal 90% credible intervals cover only about 48–64% on development questions, so the authors recommend point estimates rather than those intervals. The strongest forks can erase or reverse the smoothing advantage. Scope is limited to short multiple-choice outcomes and two model families at the same parameter scale. Open-ended tasks and long agent horizons remain untested.

Appendix E, pages 6–8, shows that most pooled improvement comes from smoothing, while segmentation matters especially near forks. Appendix G, page 9, documents a silent library fallback from a custom cost to L2. A successful software run therefore did not certify that the intended statistical model ran. Appendix H identifies autonomous-agent involvement under human direction; that provenance does not replace validation.

Reading route and capability

Read Methods on pages 2–3, Sections 3.1–3.3 on page 4, Appendix A on page 6, and Appendices E–G on pages 6–9. Inspect Figure 10 on page 11 for the distinct effects of missing segmentation and missing kernel pooling. You should explain the target estimand, branch weighting, bias–variance tradeoff, noisy references, and why pooled accuracy can hide fork-local failure. The local statistics lab illustrates inferential assumptions; it does not implement this pooling estimator or reproduce the billion-token experiment.

Exercises

  1. Why can smoothing improve average TVD while harming the most scientifically interesting positions?
  2. For independent Bernoulli estimates with $p=0.6$, $S=20$, and five genuinely stationary positions, compute raw and pooled standard errors.
  3. Specify a validation design that avoids using the same samples in estimate and reference.
