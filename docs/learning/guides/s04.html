What shape does a model give a concept?

A hidden activation is a vector, but the meaningful states a model visits need not fill vector space. The central question of “The World Inside Neural Networks” is whether understanding the geometry of those states provides a missing link between representation, computation, and reliable behavioral control. Its mountain-car example makes that question concrete; its broader claims constitute a research agenda.

Preparation: linear algebra, neural representations, interventions, statistical evidence, and geometry. The experiment chapter and local labs provide optional computational preparation.

A scalar concept need not have a straight representation

Suppose an image model tracks a car's position along a valley. Position is one scalar, so it is tempting to expect a hidden coordinate that increases monotonically along one straight direction. But the model is optimized to predict images, rather than to supply human-readable coordinates. The images change through perspective, background, and occlusion as the car moves. A curved hidden trajectory may be entirely compatible with an accurate world model.

The source trains an image-action predictor on mountain-car states and random actions. Its purpose here is next-frame prediction, rather than learning the environment's reward-maximizing policy. The hidden image embeddings form a string-like structure ordered by position. The investigators fit a one-dimensional curve and intervene along it; predicted car position changes smoothly. Straight paths can instead produce garbled images or cross a representation associated with an unintended position.

This is the consequential failure: two understandable endpoints do not guarantee an understandable interpolation. If a control method assumes a straight representation, a larger edit can leave the states on which the downstream network learned to operate. A model may then respond unpredictably even though the investigator is using a seemingly simple semantic knob.

Ambient coordinates, intrinsic coordinates, and data support

Let $h\in\mathbb R^d$ denote the activation at a chosen location in a fixed network. The ambient dimension is $d$, the number of scalar activation coordinates. A concept state is described by a smaller coordinate $z\in\mathcal Z$, where $\mathcal Z$ is a conceptual domain such as position, orientation, or month identity. An encoding map $g:\mathcal Z\to\mathbb R^d$ sends conceptual states to hidden states.

If $\mathcal Z$ locally has $k$ real coordinates and $g$ is smooth with derivative of rank $k$, its image locally forms a $k$-dimensional immersed manifold. Additional conditions are needed for an embedded manifold without self-intersections. The practical lesson is simpler: a low-dimensional set can bend through a large ambient space. Its intrinsic dimension need not equal the number of linear principal components needed to approximate it.

A concept manifold is also an idealization of finite data. Actual activations may form a noisy tube, depend on several nuisance variables, or contain branches and singularities. A fitted smooth curve summarizes observations; it does not prove the hidden state has exactly one degree of freedom. One must ask what was held fixed while the curve was collected.

The source uses “linear” in a specific sense: a concept is a direction whose magnitude gives its intensity. That is more restrictive than saying a linear probe can decode some property, and more restrictive than allowing a multidimensional linear subspace containing a curved occupied set. These meanings should stay separate when assessing the argument.

Why a straight edit accumulates error

Let $g(s)$ be a smooth curve parameterized by a scalar $s$, and suppose the current activation is $g(s_0)$. The correct small conceptual change $\Delta s$ produces

$$g(s_0+\Delta s)=g(s_0)+g'(s_0)\Delta s+\frac12g''(s_0)(\Delta s)^2+O((\Delta s)^3).$$

A tangent steering vector uses only $g'(s_0)\Delta s$. The omitted term is second order, so local linear steering can work well even when a global straight direction fails. This reconciles two observations often presented as opposites: linear methods can be powerful, and representation geometry can still matter.

For a unit circle $g(s)=(\cos s,\sin s)$, a tangent edit from $s_0=0$ reaches $(1,\Delta s)$ with norm $\sqrt{1+(\Delta s)^2}$. The true activation remains norm one. Their separation is approximately $(\Delta s)^2/2$ to leading order. With a large edit, the discrepancy is no longer small. The calculation is a statement about this specified geometry; it is not a bound on the mountain-car model's image error.

A global chord has a related problem. Between angles $\pm\alpha$ with $0\leq\alpha\leq\pi/2$, the midpoint is $(\cos\alpha,0)$ rather than $(1,0)$. Its distance from the circle is $1-\cos\alpha$. At $\alpha=\pi/2$, opposite endpoints have midpoint zero, as far from the unit circle as its radius. Endpoint validity provides no convexity guarantee.

Original worked example: a curved position code

To isolate the geometric issue, consider a synthetic position $q\in[-1,1]$ encoded by $g(q)=(q,q^2)$. This parabola is a one-dimensional manifold in two dimensions. Let a deliberately specified decoder return the position $q=h_1$ only when $|h_2-h_1^2|\leq0.1$; outside that tube it returns an invalid-state flag. This example is original teaching material, not a replication or an inferred implementation of the source model.

The endpoints for $q=-1$ and $q=1$ are $(-1,1)$ and $(1,1)$. A linear interpolation gives $h(t)=(-1+2t,1)$. At its midpoint, $h(1/2)=(0,1)$, whereas the correct encoded position is $g(0)=(0,0)$. The decoder rejects the midpoint because its consistency residual is one.

Along the chord the residual is $1-(-1+2t)^2=4t(1-t)$. Validity therefore requires $4t(1-t)\leq0.1$, which holds only near the endpoints: $t\leq(1-\sqrt{0.9})/2$ or $t\geq(1+\sqrt{0.9})/2$. In contrast, the manifold path $g(-1+2t)$ has zero consistency residual throughout. The result follows from the chosen decoder and demonstrates possibility; real decoders may tolerate, repair, or exploit off-manifold states differently.

This example also shows why coordinates and semantics must be distinguished. The first coordinate perfectly decodes position everywhere in the plane, including invalid states. A linear probe could therefore predict position accurately while missing the decoder's consistency condition. Decodability alone does not establish that arbitrary activation edits preserve valid computation.

Representation, computation, and behavior

The source distinguishes three explanatory levels. A representation is the data structure used internally. A computation transforms that structure. Behavior is the resulting observable response. Knowing one level does not determine the others. A circle can encode weekdays; it does not tell us whether the model adds offsets by phase rotation, retrieves examples, or routes through a general arithmetic mechanism.

Formally, let $T$ denote the network computation from one internal stage to another and let $R$ be an output readout. A mechanistic explanation seeks a conceptual transformation $\tau$ such that $T(g(z))\approx g'(\tau(z))$, with the appropriate encoding $g'$ at the next stage. The behavior is then $R(T(g(z)))$. Matching this relation under controlled interventions is stronger evidence than showing that $z$ can be predicted from $h$.

The source's analogy with ordinary software is useful: understanding sorting requires identifying both the stored objects and the operations acting on them. Neural geometry proposes candidate internal objects. Circuit analysis must still identify who reads and writes those objects. The geometric-calculator companion develops a concrete example of that second step.

Why world structure might reappear internally

Months have cyclic relations; nearby positions produce related images; biological sequences have shared evolutionary ancestry. Training data carry statistical traces of such relations. A predictive model may compress them by organizing hidden states accordingly. This is the source's explanatory hypothesis about how an internal ontology emerges.

The hypothesis is plausible but does not uniquely determine geometry. An invertible change of hidden coordinates can preserve behavior while changing Euclidean distances and curvature. Architectures, normalization, optimization, and regularization may privilege some coordinates, but one must specify them. “The model represents the world” is therefore weaker than “this Euclidean geometry is a uniquely identifiable conceptual structure.”

Evidence is strongest when geometry predicts held-out similarities, computations, and intervention effects that were not used to fit it. A visualization that resembles a familiar object is a useful lead. It is insufficient on its own because dimensionality reduction can distort neighborhoods, discard nuisance variation, or emphasize a selected factor.

What changes when using sparse autoencoders

An SAE represents an activation as a sparse weighted sum of learned directions. Such a dictionary can approximate a curved manifold, but separate atoms may label only local neighborhoods. The source's slant-rhyme example contrasts a continuous organization of phonological endings with scattered automated labels for individual SAE features. Locally accurate labels can obscure a global phonological factor.

This is not a mathematical impossibility result for SAEs. Several directions can jointly parameterize a circle or more complicated surface. The issue is the match between the training objective, the recovered decomposition, and the researcher's interpretive unit. A dictionary optimized for reconstruction and sparsity need not recover the most convenient global coordinates. The SAE geometry companion distinguishes shattering, compact capture, and dilution; BSFs replace individual selection with block selection.

It is also possible for direct neurons to be interpretable while residual-stream representations form manifolds. These claims concern different objects. Nothing in the geometry agenda logically requires every native neuron to be an arbitrary mixture, and nothing in neuron legibility rules out curved combinations.

Evidence audit and open commitments

The mountain-car intervention supports a causal relation between a fitted hidden curve and predicted position in that controlled system. The source gathers other geometric examples from its own work and cited literature, but the broad promise of robust control, better models, and comprehensive understanding remains an agenda. A small world model does not establish a universal method for truthful or safe language generation.

Useful controls would compare curved and linear paths at matched edit strength, test unseen positions and actions, preserve momentum or other variables, and quantify image quality independently of position. For language models, a concept's apparent curve may change with wording, layer, or task. A robust controller must handle that context dependence.

The central inference worth retaining is conditional: when meaningful states occupy a curved set and the downstream computation depends on staying near it, geometry-aware edits can outperform straight edits. The task for research is to identify where those conditions hold, how to estimate the geometry, and which invariants survive changes of context.

Exercises

  1. Conceptual: Explain how a property can be linearly decodable while a straight edit to that property fails.
  2. Mathematical: For $g(q)=(q,q^2)$ on $q\in[-1,1]$, compare the chord between $q=-a$ and $q=a$ with the manifold at its midpoint, and derive the discrepancy for $0\leq a\leq1$.
  3. Experimental: Design a mountain-car control test separating position steering from accidental changes in momentum or image quality.
