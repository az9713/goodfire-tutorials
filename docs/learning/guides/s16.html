Addition as a computation on circles

The question is stronger than “does a model represent numbers geometrically?” It is whether identified circuitry computes with those representations and reuses the computation across ordinary arithmetic, weekdays, and months. The source studies an addition module in layer 18 of Llama 3.1 8B and combines activation patterns with steering interventions.

Preparation: bilinear operations, layers and neurons, causal interventions, Fourier coordinates. Related: manifold steering.

What a modular circle represents

For integer $n$ and positive integer period $m$, define $\phi_m(n)=(\cos(2\pi n/m),\sin(2\pi n/m))$. Numbers differing by a multiple of $m$ share a point. This is a circular encoding of the residue $n\bmod m$. Concatenating several periods gives a richer representation but not automatically a unique encoding of every integer.

The source describes number representations using multiple circular Fourier components, including periods that are not coprime. Redundancy is not a contradiction: a parity component can separate neighboring numbers that lie close together on a long-period circle. The source offers this as a possible reason for the redundancy, rather than a settled optimization theorem.

If all periods divide $M$, the joint representation repeats after $M$; more generally it repeats after their least common multiple. A readout can identify an integer only within an appropriate range or with additional context. The fact that modular addition is exact does not remove the ambiguity between, for example, 14 and 114 when all represented periods divide 100.

Deriving addition in Fourier coordinates

Write input coordinates as $\phi_m(a)=(c_a,s_a)$ and $\phi_m(b)=(c_b,s_b)$. The trigonometric addition identities give

$$\phi_m(a+b)=(c_ac_b-s_as_b,\ s_ac_b+c_as_b).$$

Equivalently, represent each pair as a complex unit number $u_m(n)=e^{2\pi i n/m}$; then $u_m(a+b)=u_m(a)u_m(b)$. Thus addition of integers becomes multiplication of phases. These are exact mathematical identities. They explain why circular representations are suitable for addition, but do not establish that the studied neurons implement this particular multiplication algorithm. The source's network-specific claim rests on its measured circuit behavior and interventions.

The distinction matters because several neural implementations can realize the same map: lookup-like nonlinearities, distributed bilinear approximations, or more elaborate mixtures. Recognizing the input-output geometry narrows the mechanism without uniquely identifying every internal operation.

Original worked example: a calendar interface

This teaching example uses a clean mathematical system, not model measurements. Number months January $=1$ through December $=12$. September plus five months gives $9+5=14$, whose month is $1+((14-1)\bmod12)=2$, February. On a period-12 circle, September has phase $3\pi/2$ and five months corresponds to phase $5\pi/6$. Their sum is $7\pi/3$, equivalent to $\pi/3$, the phase for month 2.

A shared numeric module can process the sum while a task-specific input mapping converts September to 9 and an output mapping converts 14 to February. These interfaces are part of the full mechanism. A common middle representation alone does not show that the entire calendar and arithmetic computations are identical. Testing reuse should target the putative shared module while checking that the task-specific interfaces remain functional.

Causal evidence with a qualification

The source follows information across layers and token positions, observes periodic neuron patterns across tasks, and steers the putative sum along several circles simultaneously. Changing the downstream predicted month gives causal support that these representations participate in the computation. Correlational visualization alone could have shown a by-product rather than a used variable.

An explicit source footnote limits the intervention: the circle magnitudes are increased to many times naturally occurring values. Such amplification may reveal an available readout while changing the operating regime. It supports a causal connection but leaves questions about natural-scale necessity, redundancy, and quantitative contribution. Selective ablation, natural-range interchange interventions, and specificity controls would strengthen the mechanistic account.

The downloaded explainer is a compact case study, not proof that all arithmetic in all models uses one Fourier calculator. Its strongest lesson is methodological: understand the representation, identify the operation, and then test their causal link.

Exercises

  1. Conceptual: Why does a circular activation plot not by itself demonstrate addition?
  2. Mathematical: If periods are $2,5,10$, what is the joint repetition period? Can those circles distinguish 14 from 24?
  3. Critical experiment: Design a natural-scale test of a shared module across addition and month questions.
