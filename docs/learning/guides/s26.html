Use internal signals as affordable supervision

Fact checking every claim with a search-enabled judge is expensive. The February 11, 2026 article proposes Reinforcement Learning from Feature Rewards (RLFR): train probes on model activations, use them to detect hallucinated entity claims and score repairs, and use those scores as reinforcement-learning rewards. Its system also inserts corrections during generation. Optional preparation: network gradients, classifier evaluation, and intentional design.

Separate detection, repair, and learning

The source trains a localization probe to find candidate entity spans, a classifier to flag hallucinations, and separate reward probes for corrections and retractions. Labels come from factually dense Gemma rollouts judged using Gemini 2.5 with web search. Probes make expensive labels reusable; they do not remove the need for factual supervision or create an oracle of truth.

During reinforcement learning, reward probes operate on a frozen copy of the base model processing the student's output. Let $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ be the student response distribution, $H_0(x,y)$ frozen-model activations, and $R(x,y)=r(H_0(x,y))$ the learned reward. The expected-reward gradient is

$$\nabla_\theta\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}R=\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\left[(R-c(x))\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\right],$$

where $c(x)$ is an action-independent variance-reduction baseline. There is no path for the student to improve reward merely by rotating its own activations while leaving tokens fixed. It can still exploit blind spots in how the frozen model and probe evaluate those tokens. Freezing protects a representation interface, not the correctness of the reward.

Original worked example: the pipeline's arithmetic

Imagine 1,000 independently labeled claims containing 200 hallucinations. A detector with 80% recall flags 160 of them. If its precision is also 80%, it flags 200 claims total, including 40 true claims. Suppose a repair step successfully fixes or appropriately retracts 75% of the 160 genuine hallucinations and accidentally corrupts 10% of the 40 true claims. Residual hallucinations are $200-120+4=84$, a 58% relative reduction.

This deliberately invented example happens to match the article's headline percentage numerically; it is not its experimental decomposition. It illustrates that detection, intervention quality, and collateral damage jointly determine the system outcome. A perfect repair policy cannot fix undetected claims, and a high-recall detector can harm truthful output if false positives are handled poorly.

For best-of-$n$ repair candidates, if each independently succeeds with probability $p$ and a perfect selector recognizes success, the chance of at least one success is $1-(1-p)^n$. With $p=0.2$ and $n=4$, it is 0.5904. Real candidates are correlated and the selector imperfect, so this formula is an optimistic structural model, not a predicted scaling law for RLFR.

Read the headline with its harness

The source's 58% hallucination reduction applies to Gemma-3-12B-IT with the probing/intervention harness and best-of-32 sampling on a held-out LongFact++ test set of 999 prompts. It combines policy changes, in-context effects of inserted corrections, direct fixes, and test-time selection. It should not be reported as “RL alone makes the unassisted model hallucinate 58% less.” The article separately describes policy reduction, in-context reduction, and direct reduction, and includes a trained-policy condition without inline interventions.

The reported training involved about 360 optimizer steps and roughly $2,500; its approximately 90-fold cost comparison concerns the studied per-intervention supervision alternative. Those are dated experimental estimates and depend on the harness, batching, labeling, and model costs. The claim of no degradation concerns the standard benchmarks tested, not every capability or every mode of uncertainty expression.

What could still go wrong?

Entity-level classification can miss relational errors, misleading omissions, or well-formed claims about obscure subjects. Retraction can reduce false statements by saying less, so factual precision should be paired with coverage, informativeness, and appropriate abstention. A judge-derived benchmark also inherits label errors and correlated preferences from its judge.

The source reports that probes remain useful on the trained policy, including at test time. This is evidence against one feared collapse of the probe signal under the studied optimization, not a guarantee under stronger or adversarial optimization. Its hypotheses about why the model fails to use available factual information are plausible alternatives: uncertainty about the task, post-training incentives for fluency, or weak coupling between checking and generation. The experiments do not uniquely identify which mechanism dominates.

To strengthen the result, compare equal-cost external judging, frozen and student-activation rewards, and matched test-time sampling budgets. Use independent fact checking and hold out entire domains or entity families. Track truthful information delivered per response alongside hallucination rates. A scaled local lab can illustrate reward selection without claiming to repeat the published model training.

Exercises

  1. Concept. Why can probe transfer after training be encouraging without proving that rewards cannot be hacked?
  2. Math. In the invented claim pipeline, lower repair success from 75% to 50%. Compute residual errors and relative reduction.
  3. Critique. How would you distinguish better factuality from a model simply avoiding factual content?
