Prompts and steering as two ways to change odds

Can one compact model explain both many-shot in-context learning and activation steering? The paper proposes a Bayesian account: examples supply evidence for a latent concept, while steering shifts its prior log odds. The model predicts sigmoidal behavioral changes and approximately additive effects in log-odds space.

Preparation: activation editing, interventions, Bayes factors and logistic curves. Compare curved steering for situations where a global straight intervention may fail.

A latent concept with two controls

Let $c$ denote a target concept and $c'$ its complement. Let $x_{1:N}$ be $N$ contextual examples and $m\in\mathbb R$ a steering magnitude. Define the target posterior log odds $\eta=\log[P(c\mid x)/P(c'\mid x)]$. Bayes' rule gives

$$\eta=\log\frac{P(c)}{P(c')}+\log\frac{P(x_{1:N}\mid c)}{P(x_{1:N}\mid c')}.$$

For independent equally informative examples, the second term is a sum of log likelihood ratios and grows linearly with $N$. The paper instead models discounted evidence growth, motivated by empirical scaling with repeated context. Write the resulting model in an explicit positive-target convention as

$$\eta(N,m)=b+\gamma N^{1-\alpha}+am,\qquad P(c\mid N,m)=\sigma(\eta),$$

where $b$ is baseline log odds, $\gamma$ controls evidence strength, $\alpha$ controls discounting, $a$ controls steering sensitivity, and $\sigma(u)=1/(1+e^{-u})$. For increasing but sublinear evidence take $0\lt\alpha\lt1$ and $\gamma\gt0$. A mapping from concept belief to measured output behavior is an additional assumption.

The downloaded PDF has apparent sign inconsistencies in some displayed sigmoid equations on pages 5–6. The convention here follows the unambiguous identity $P(c)=\sigma(\log[P(c)/P(c')])$ and defines positive $a$ as steering toward $c$. This avoids importing a sign ambiguity into the derivation.

Why sharp behavioral transitions need no discontinuous mechanism

The two controls add before the sigmoid. At a fixed $N$, steering changes log odds by $a\Delta m$. At fixed $m$, more examples add discounted evidence. The probability can change rapidly near $\eta=0$ because $d\sigma/d\eta=\sigma(1-\sigma)$ peaks there. Saturation near zero and one makes additional interventions look ineffective even when their log-odds effect is unchanged.

The half-probability boundary satisfies $b+\gamma N^{1-\alpha}+am=0$. When $-b-am\gt0$, its solution is $N_*=[(-b-am)/\gamma]^{1/(1-\alpha)}$. A small steering shift can move this boundary across many examples. This is a quantitative prediction of the fitted model, not evidence of a literal phase transition in network parameters.

The interpretation of steering as a prior shift is useful when the intervention contributes a context-independent offset. It is not uniquely identified by output probabilities alone: changing a likelihood by the same constant can generate the same posterior odds. The paper motivates the prior interpretation through linear representation assumptions and intervention behavior.

Original worked example: a harmless style concept

Use a target concept “answer in a concise style,” with $b=-4$, $\gamma=1$, $\alpha=1/2$, and $a=2$. These are invented teaching parameters, not fitted source results. Without steering, $\eta=-4+\sqrt N$ and the midpoint occurs at $N=16$. At $m=1$, it occurs at $N=4$. At $N=9$, probabilities are $\sigma(-1)\approx0.269$ without steering and $\sigma(1)\approx0.731$ with steering.

These changes are symmetric around one half in this example because the logits are $-1$ and $1$. Adding the two probability gains would be incorrect. The additive variables are log odds, not probabilities. If the measured outcome is a mixture of behaviors under several concepts, even the final sigmoid mapping may need extension.

Evidence and failure modes

The paper tests many-shot persona tasks, a flipped-label sentiment task, and several models, with held-out prediction across intervention magnitudes. Its selected tasks permit a measurable transition within the tested context range. This makes the quantitative model testable, while limiting claims about arbitrary instruction following or genuinely novel algorithms.

Fitted agreement does not establish that the network explicitly stores Bayesian hypotheses or computes a scalar posterior. Alternative latent models can have similar response curves. The source also warns that extreme steering can leave the support where its assumptions hold. Large-magnitude incoherence should not be treated as a failure of Bayes' rule; it can be a failure of the representation model used to connect the intervention to log odds.

Exercises

  1. Conceptual: Why is additivity in log odds compatible with strong nonadditivity in probabilities?
  2. Mathematical: In the worked example, find $m$ needed for probability $1/2$ when $N=1$.
  3. Critical experiment: How would you test whether steering contributes a context-independent offset?
