Choose the tool around the question

The September 28, 2026 practical guide revises a common expectation: sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are useful for broad exploration, but usually unnecessary when a known concept has labeled examples. It distinguishes an unsupervised concept map from a supervised detector and explains the infrastructure required to build a useful map. Optional preparation: SAE mathematics and prediction and validation.

Unknown questions versus known targets

An SAE learns sparse coordinates from activations without being told which concepts matter. This lets investigators notice unexpected representations, compare models, and form new hypotheses. A probe is a classifier trained on examples of a predetermined concept. It can devote its capacity to that concept rather than fitting the entire activation distribution. The guide recommends supervised probes for known monitoring and screening tasks, and notes that SAE-based steering often underperforms simpler alternatives.

These are practical recommendations based on the comparisons cited in the article, not impossibility theorems. An SAE can impose useful regularization, support interpretation of a probe direction, or reveal a concept before labels exist. The decisive issue is performance on the intended use, including cost, coverage, calibration, and shift.

Why reconstruction quality matters for detection

Let $X\in\mathbb R^d$ be an activation, $Z=E(X)$ its deterministic SAE encoding, and $Y$ a target label. Any predictor using only $Z$ is also a possible predictor using $X$: compose the encoder with that predictor. Hence, with unlimited data, computation, and an unrestricted predictor class, the best attainable prediction risk from $X$ cannot exceed that from $Z$. This is a function-class argument; it does not say a finite raw-activation probe always wins in practice.

A lossy encoder can discard a target-relevant component while retaining most activation variance. Write $X=\hat X+\epsilon$, with $\hat X=DZ+b$ the reconstruction and $\epsilon$ its residual. A detector based on $Z$ cannot necessarily recover information carried only in $\epsilon$. Low reconstruction error averaged over all dimensions does not ensure the residual is irrelevant to a particular safety target.

Original counterexample: tiny error, lost label

Let $U$ and $Y$ be independent, each equally likely to be $-1$ or $+1$. Define activation $X=(10U,0.1Y)$ and a toy encoder $Z=U$. Reconstruct as $\hat X=(10U,0)$. Expected squared reconstruction error is 0.01, compared with total activation energy 100.01, so the explained-energy fraction is about 99.99%.

Nevertheless, $Z$ is independent of $Y$, so no feature-only classifier beats chance on the label. The raw second coordinate predicts $Y$ perfectly by its sign. This is not a model of a real SAE result; it proves that excellent reconstruction in an aggregate energy metric does not logically imply excellent detection of every concept.

A useful interpreter requires more than a loss curve

The guide's pipeline starts with the hook site: which layer and location supplies activations. Early, middle, and late layers can contain different information; one SAE generally does not transfer reliably across layers or models. Activation data must cover the use case. A coding-agent audit needs agent trajectories, not merely generic prose.

Dictionary width sets how many potential directions exist; sparsity controls how many are active. Increasing width can reveal finer concepts but also split concepts into narrower fragments. Increasing active-feature budgets improves reconstruction while potentially reducing interpretability. Dead features waste capacity. Architecture choices such as TopK, BatchTopK, and JumpReLU change this tradeoff rather than abolishing it.

After training, labels require a separate autointerpretation step. An LLM sees activating examples and proposes a description. Validate that description on held-out examples, including moderate activations and adversarial confounders. Top examples can be coherent even when the feature has a second common use. Label quality is an empirical object, not a property guaranteed by sparsity.

What the guide establishes and leaves open

This is a practical synthesis with links to external studies and product demonstrations, rather than a single controlled benchmark. Its examples span auditing, model diffing, data analysis, scientific discovery, and interpretation of other tools. Their shared argument is that imperfect maps can be valuable for finding leads. An exploratory hypothesis becomes a scientific result only after independent testing.

The article's table simplifies black-box methods, probes, and SAEs into broad categories. Judge accuracy and cost depend strongly on task and deployment setting; probes require representative labels; unsupervised exploration still depends on activation coverage and assumptions. The advertised Silico workflow is an optional product route, not an essential component of this offline course or evidence that every scientific judgment is automated.

For a solo experiment, begin with existing local activations and compare a raw linear probe, an SAE-feature probe, and a simple baseline under identical splits. Reserve a separate exploratory analysis to inspect unknown patterns. Keeping those objectives distinct prevents a beautiful map from being mistaken for a validated detector. Exercises

  1. Concept. How can an SAE help build a monitor even if the final monitor should be a supervised probe?
  2. Math. Replace 0.1 in the toy activation by $\delta\gt0$. Derive relative reconstruction error and explain its limit.
  3. Experiment. Design a comparison where an SAE-feature probe could beat a raw linear probe without contradicting the information argument.
