Three ways to retrieve a bound entity

If a model reads “Mira owns a kite,” how does it later retrieve kite when asked about Mira? “Mixing Mechanisms” argues that position alone is insufficient. It separates positional, lexical, and reflexive retrieval using counterfactual activation swaps designed so that the mechanisms predict different answers.

Preparation: causal attention and residual streams, interchange interventions, distribution comparisons.

When ordinary accuracy cannot identify a mechanism

A binding associates entities filling different roles in a clause. In a normal prompt, all three candidate mechanisms can retrieve the same target. A positional mechanism stores which group to revisit. A lexical mechanism stores a query entity and finds its associated target. A reflexive mechanism carries a pointer to the target itself, which must still be resolved against the context.

Autoregressive attention constrains the available routes. Earlier tokens cannot attend to later tokens while their representations are first computed. If a target occurs before its bound query, a proposed mechanism cannot simply write the later query backward into that earlier state. This architectural fact motivates a distinction between a query-based lookup and a previously retrieved pointer. It does not uniquely determine every implementation.

The paper finds positional information more concentrated at the ends of lists and more diffuse in middle groups. Lexical and reflexive contributions depend on the ordering of entities within a group. Thus a single retrieval strategy inferred from short lists can be incomplete in longer, more varied settings.

Original worked example: force the hypotheses to disagree

Take an original context: “Ava owns a kite. Ben owns a mug. Cy owns a drum. Dee owns a lamp. What does Dee own?” The ordinary answer is lamp. Construct a counterfactual: “Ben owns a kite. Ava owns a drum. Cy owns a mug. Dee owns a lamp. What does Ava own?” Its answer is drum and its queried group is second.

After transferring the counterfactual retrieval signal into the original run, positional retrieval predicts mug, from the original second group. Lexical retrieval predicts kite, associated with Ava in the original. Reflexive retrieval predicts drum, the counterfactual target that remains present in the original. Unchanged behavior predicts lamp. Four distinct outcomes make the experiment informative. This example is original teaching material using the paper's logic, not its reported prompt pair.

A further ambiguity remains: a drum output might result from copying an already computed answer rather than transferring a pointer. Replace drum in the counterfactual answer with an entity absent from the original. A pointer that requires dereferencing should fail to retrieve that absent item, while a copied answer can still produce it. The source uses this additional design and compares adjacent layers to address the distinction.

Combining signals at the logit level

Let $i$ index candidate entity groups. A simplified version of the paper's causal model assigns score

$$s_i=w_P\,\varphi(i;i_P,\sigma_P)+w_L(i_L)\mathbf1_{i=i_L}+w_R(i_R)\mathbf1_{i=i_R},$$

where $i_P,i_L,i_R$ are the three proposed target indices, $\varphi$ is a Gaussian-shaped positional profile, $\sigma_P$ is its width, and the $w$ terms are learned strengths. Probabilities are $p_i=e^{s_i}/\sum_j e^{s_j}$. This is additive evidence in scores, not an arithmetic mixture of three output distributions. A sharp lexical peak can overcome a broad positional signal even when the latter has substantial total mass.

To compare predicted distribution $p$ with measured distribution $q$, define $m=(p+q)/2$ and Jensen–Shannon divergence $\operatorname{JSD}(p,q)=[\operatorname{KL}(p\|m)+\operatorname{KL}(q\|m)]/2$. With base-two logarithms, the paper's similarity is $1-\operatorname{JSD}$ and lies in $[0,1]$. A value such as 0.95 is distributional similarity, not 95 percent answer accuracy. This distinction prevents overreading the abstract's compressed agreement language.

Evidence and remaining scope

The paper tests counterfactuals across model families, sizes, and binding templates, fits a three-mechanism causal model, and ablates terms. It also interleaves longer free-form filler text. These are stronger tests than merely identifying an attention head that looks back at an entity.

Intervention states can still be unusual combinations of signals. Predicting them supports the causal abstraction in the tested setting, while ordinary long-context reasoning may involve repeated names, coreference, changing attributes, and many-to-many relations absent from the distinct-entity templates. The model's Gaussian positional term is a useful fitted approximation rather than a literal anatomical component. More filler text is a step toward naturalism, not an unrestricted test of real documents.

Exercises

  1. Conceptual: Why do ordinary correct answers fail to distinguish the three mechanisms?
  2. Mathematical: For scores $(0,\log2,\log4)$, compute output probabilities. What happens if the same constant is added to every score?
  3. Critical experiment: How would you distinguish a reflexive pointer from an already copied answer?
