Source 02 · Nick Cammarata's research essay and empirical argument

Are neurons tangled, or are our explanations too crude?

“Neural Networks Are Already Clean” challenges a consequential premise: that native neurons are so arbitrarily mixed that interpretability must first replace them with a learned decomposition. Cammarata argues that improved tools, modern architectures, training trajectories, and causal investigations often reveal coherent native mechanisms. He proposes recursive generalization as an explanation for much apparent polysemanticity. His broader optimism about solving interpretability remains a conjecture.

Preparation: basis and readout, neurons, residual streams, and gates, causal tests, measurement and sampling, sparse representations. Use experiment literacy when designing the tests below.

The disputed premise

A neuron may fire on cats and cars. One explanation is arbitrary packing: unrelated features share scarce capacity because they rarely occur together. Another is a common pattern that the observer has missed. These explanations suggest different research strategies. Packing motivates an untangling model, such as an SAE. A common pattern motivates closer study of the original neuron's inputs, outputs, training history, and counterexamples.

The essay distinguishes these possibilities through cases rather than a universal theorem. Its “clean” does not mean every neuron has one ordinary noun as its label. It means the native mechanisms often contain useful, coherent distinctions that remain understandable when examined in their circuit context. A neuron can have related offshoots and still be scientifically legible.

Keep the terminology precise. Polysemanticity describes apparent multiple meanings. Superposition can mean more linearly encoded features than ambient dimensions, or it can mean the learning principle of packing unrelated features. Cammarata mainly disputes the latter as a dominant explanation of native-neuron mixtures. A counting statement about features and dimensions does not settle that dispute.

Revisiting cats, cars, and misleading microscopes

In “Cars and Animals,” the famous InceptionV1 cat-and-car neuron is reinterpreted as responding to a V-shaped pattern. Positive and negative examples, circuit neighbors, and inputs from flames and water jets provide evidence that a list of object categories missed a shared shape. Its negative pole supplies a further constraint: rounded patterns oppose pointed ones. This is a source-reported interpretation, not a proof that every response has been exhausted.

The essay similarly revisits a car-to-dog circuit previously interpreted as hidden storage. Its proposed explanation is shared pose and ordinary composition. Feature visualization had favored dog-like images because optimization found an easy route to high activation. An optimized image therefore answers “what input can raise this scalar under these constraints?” It need not reveal a unique semantic essence or the natural input distribution.

“A History of Microscope Errors” separates tool failure from model learning failure. Improved visualization can clarify the same fixed checkpoint; architecture and training changes can alter what neurons learn. These interventions should not be conflated. A cleaner modern network is evidence against inevitability of messiness, but it does not by itself establish what the older network was doing.

Recursive generalization and capacity pressure

Recursive generalization means a neuron expands from one useful pattern into locally related patterns, which can themselves generate further offshoots. The final collection can look disconnected when viewed only through human labels. The essay's spelling example proceeds from “iy” through “diy” to “do it yourself.” The final phrase lacks the original character sequence, yet the developmental route is intelligible.

Training trajectories matter because a plausible after-the-fact story is easy to invent. Watching activation families appear over checkpoints constrains which developmental explanations are possible. Still, temporal order is not sufficient for causality: correlated patterns may grow together because of a third input or shared gradient. A strong test combines trajectories with changed data, targeted counterexamples, and interventions on the proposed pathways.

The essay tests capacity pressure by narrowing layers and retraining. It reports broader, coarser concepts rather than a sudden switch to arbitrary mixtures. It also compares differently sized ConvNeXt models and investigates learned dictionaries. These findings challenge a simple prediction that insufficient space inevitably produces unrelated native-neuron packing. They leave open different regimes, tasks, architectures, and representational locations where interference may matter.

The essay's comparisons with SAEs ask whether dictionary features reveal fundamental building blocks absent from native neurons. Its reported examples often look like contextual groupings of existing mechanisms. Such a result is relevant to those models and layers; it is not a general impossibility theorem for useful decomposition. The geometry companion and BSF companion address different situations and should be compared at the level of assumptions and measurements.

What a neuron does to downstream computation

For input vector $x\in\mathbb R^d$, a simple neuron has preactivation $u=w^\top x+b$, activation $a=\max(0,u)$, and output contribution $av$, where $w\in\mathbb R^d$ is its input weight, $b$ its bias, and $v$ its downstream write vector. Interpreting only top activating inputs studies $a$; interpreting its circuit requires studying both $w$ and $v$ and the downstream readers of $v$.

For a downstream scalar score $y=F(h)$ with residual activation $h$, a small intervention $\Delta a$ changes $h$ by $v\Delta a$. First-order expansion gives

$$\Delta y\approx\nabla_hF(h)^\top v\,\Delta a.$$

The same activation magnitude can have very different consequences depending on the readout gradient. A neuron can detect a pattern without strongly affecting the particular output under study. Conversely, a small activation can matter when its downstream sensitivity is large or when the output is near a decision boundary.

This explains the essay's emphasis on control flow and positive/negative distinctions. In a ReLU neuron, negative preactivations are clipped, but inhibitory input weights can still determine which inputs reach the active regime. “Negative pole” here can refer to preactivation structure; it should not be confused with a negative post-ReLU output.

Original worked example: two descriptions that fit the same observations

Consider four binary properties: cat identity $C$, car identity $A$, pointed shape $V$, and round shape $O$. Define a synthetic neuron's preactivation as $u=2V-2O-1$ and output as $a=\max(0,u)$. Suppose the observed corpus contains only pointed cat faces, pointed car fronts, and round non-cat/non-car objects. Both “cat-or-car detector” and “pointed-shape detector” predict the observed activations.

Now test a pointed flame with $(C,A,V,O)=(0,0,1,0)$. The true synthetic neuron gives $a=1$, while the category explanation predicts no activation. A round car with $(0,1,0,1)$ produces $a=0$, contradicting the category explanation in the opposite direction. The matched counterexamples break a correlation that made both stories seem adequate. This is original teaching material inspired by the essay's methodological issue, not a recreation of its V neuron.

To add use, let a downstream score be $y=3a+z$, where $z$ is another pathway. Ablating $a$ changes $y$ by $-3a$. If a separate classifier already makes the correct decision through $z$, the label may remain unchanged. Lack of an accuracy change therefore does not prove the neuron is unused. Measure the relevant logit or probability as well as the final answer.

The example exposes the hinge of an explanatory test: choose inputs on which competing mechanisms disagree. A more detailed label is not necessarily better; it is better only when it supports accurate predictions and survives informative attempts to falsify it.

Weak activations, raw scales, and rare mechanisms

“Do Weak Activations Matter?” separates three questions: what produces weak responses, whether they matter causally, and whether they are needed to label a neuron. These questions have different answers. A broad low-amplitude tail can arise from related analogies, yet still affect a near-tie decision. Removing weak activity with little aggregate benchmark cost does not prove no rare context depends on it.

Percentiles can conceal a neuron's natural operating scale. If one neuron is almost always near zero, its high percentile may still be tiny relative to its meaningful responses. Another neuron may function as a continuously varying dial. A useful audit reports raw magnitudes, empirical frequency, intervention effect, and context rather than treating one percentile cutoff as universal.

For a rare event with true probability $p$, the chance that $N$ independent test examples miss it is $(1-p)^N$. If $p=10^{-4}$ and $N=1000$, this is approximately $e^{-0.1}$, about $0.905$. This original calculation illustrates why a benign average effect gives weak evidence about sufficiently rare failures. It does not show that any particular hidden failure exists.

Language motifs and the sign of a gate

“Exploring Language Models” reports native-neuron attribution graphs, negative-pole examples, expert specialization, and explanation tests. Its neuron-audit percentages are preliminary and depend on thresholds, samples, and what counts as one job. They should be read as the author's regime assessment, not a universal measured constant of language models.

A concrete architectural point is SwiGLU. In a simplified scalar form, $a=\operatorname{SiLU}(g)v$, where $g$ is a gate preactivation, $v$ a value, and $\operatorname{SiLU}(g)=g\sigma(g)$ with logistic sigmoid $\sigma(g)=1/(1+e^{-g})$. Because the sigmoid is positive, SiLU has the sign of $g$. Positive products can therefore arise from positive gate and value or from both being negative.

For example, $g=-1$ and $v=-4$ give $a=4/(1+e)\approx1.076$, while $g=1$ and $v=1$ give $a=e/(1+e)\approx0.731$. A top-positive list can mix two routes with different semantics. The source argues that separating sign regimes resolves some apparent mixtures. This is a specific reason to inspect implementation details before declaring the neuron arbitrary.

The essay's “blind maximum” test asks one investigator to explain a neuron and another, seeing only the explanation, to create a strongly activating example. Success tests the proposed center of meaning. It does not test complete breadth. The source acknowledges this and supplements it with upstream bundles and other targeted tests. A highly activating sentence can validate one pathway while missing another job.

From a feature inventory to exploratory science

“Zooming Out” contrasts an enumerative program, which seeks a list of all features, with exploratory interpretability, which begins from questions and follows native mechanisms. The essay favors maps whose high-level abstractions remain connected to actual neurons and circuits. Its crossword analogy expresses a hypothesis about research productivity: explaining neighbors can constrain an ambiguous unit and make later explanations easier.

That productivity claim is plausible and testable. Compare investigators given local examples alone with investigators also given independently verified neighboring mechanisms. Measure held-out explanation accuracy, causal prediction, and time spent, with matched tools and budgets. Without such controls, improved performance may reflect better agents or more computation rather than a special compounding effect of native mechanisms.

The source is candid that attention is less understood and that enormous language models are not fully mapped. Coherent neuron labels do not by themselves establish a complete causal account, safety assurance, or reliable predictions under distribution shift. A locally readable network can still implement a globally complicated algorithm.

The productive conclusion is methodological: native neurons deserve serious investigation, and tool or category errors should be live alternatives when they look messy. The stronger proposition that this approach supplies most of a near-term solution to interpretability is explicitly speculative. Compare the essay with superposition and adversarial vulnerability and open problems without declaring the dispute settled by either title.

Exercises

  1. Conceptual: Distinguish apparent polysemanticity, more features than dimensions, and the arbitrary-packing learning hypothesis. Which does the essay primarily dispute?
  2. Mathematical: In a SwiGLU scalar, choose $g=-2$ and $v=-3$. Calculate the output and explain why a positive-only activation browser can mislead.
  3. Critical experiment: Design a preregistered test separating recursive generalization from arbitrary packing under capacity pressure.
