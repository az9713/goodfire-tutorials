When is an answer available before its rationale?

A reasoning model may generate a long explanation even when its eventual multiple-choice answer is already predictable from its internal activations. This March 2026 essay calls the mismatch “performative reasoning.” It does not equate the mismatch with deception. The practical question is whether a calibrated internal readout can stop generation early while preserving useful accuracy.

Prerequisites: classification over activations and calibration and decision theory. At reasoning position $t$, let $h_{1:t}$ be the observed activation history and $A$ the model's eventual answer. An attention probe predicts $q_t(a)=P_{\mathrm{probe}}(A=a\mid h_{1:t})$. This target is the eventual answer, which may be wrong. Distinguish prediction of the model from correctness against the benchmark's gold answer.

Original teaching derivation: an early-exit decision

Let $q$ be the calibrated probability that an immediate answer is correct. Suppose continuing costs $c$ in a chosen utility unit, a wrong answer costs $L$, and continued reasoning would achieve correctness probability $q'$. The expected losses are $L(1-q)$ for stopping and $c+L(1-q')$ for continuing. Therefore stop when

$$L(q'-q)\leq c.$$

This criterion depends on expected improvement, not confidence alone. A fixed threshold is a convenient approximation when improvement is predictably small above that threshold. It is not a universal optimum. The units of token cost and error cost must be commensurate.

For an original numerical case, take $q=0.95$, $q'=0.97$, $L=100$, and $c=1$. Stopping costs five units; continuing costs four, so continue. If continuation instead costs three, continuing costs six and stopping is preferable. A 95% confidence threshold cannot answer both decisions without the application's cost structure.

The available $q_t$ may predict agreement with the eventual model answer rather than gold correctness. A probe that predicts an incorrect final answer with 99% confidence has succeeded at its supervised target while failing the user's task. Early-exit evaluation must therefore measure final task accuracy directly, along with agreement, calibration, and token savings.

Three observation channels

“Methods” compares activation probes, forced answering at intermediate prefixes, and a separate LLM that infers the answer from written chain of thought. Agreement between probes and forced answers strengthens the evidence that answer information is available early. It still does not prove the remaining computation is causally irrelevant: forced answering changes the prompt, while a probe is an externally trained decoder.

“Results” reports much stronger early answer prediction for attention probes than single-token linear probes. This distinction matters because pooling can integrate information distributed over a context. Easier MMLU recall tasks show a larger gap between activation-based predictions and text-based monitoring than harder GPQA questions. That supports a task-relative phenomenon, not a blanket claim that chain of thought is theatrical.

The early-exit table reports DeepSeek-R1 token savings of 67.7% on MMLU with 96.7% of baseline accuracy, and 33.4% on GPQA with 100.8% of baseline accuracy. “Percent of baseline” is a ratio, not an absolute accuracy. Values slightly above 100% can arise because continued reasoning sometimes changes an answer adversely or because of finite-sample variation; they do not establish a general accuracy improvement.

Verbal inflections occur more often in lower-confidence responses but do not reliably coincide with local shifts in probe confidence. Response-level association and token-level timing are different hypotheses. A word like “Wait” cannot be assumed to mark an internal causal transition.

Limits include multiple-choice format, training-target alignment, possible label or option-position shortcuts, and distribution shift. A convincing follow-up permutes answer labels, holds out subjects, and tests whether thresholds retain calibration on genuinely harder questions. This work demonstrates decoding and adaptive-compute applications, not a complete causal account of reasoning.

Reading route and capability

Read “Methods,” each result subheading, the early-exit table, and the final two paragraphs of “Takeaways.” You should explain final-answer prediction versus correctness, forced-answer interventions versus passive probes, and confidence thresholds versus optimal stopping. Compare forking analysis for distributions over counterfactual continuations.

Exercises

  1. Why does early decodability not prove that later reasoning is useless?
  2. Baseline accuracy is 80%; early exit retains 96.7% of baseline. Find new accuracy and the percentage-point loss.
  3. Design a test for option-label leakage in an answer probe.
