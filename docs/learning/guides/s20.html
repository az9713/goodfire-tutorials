Does an evaluation change the behavior it measures?

A safety evaluation aims to estimate behavior in relevant real situations. If its wording tells the model that it is a test, the measured behavior may instead reflect a response to being tested. This May 2026 study distinguishes naturally occurring verbalizations of evaluation awareness, their association with refusal, and interventions on reasoning text. It also asks whether reducing the verbalization actually restores the intended scenario.

Prerequisites: conditioning, confounding, and causal effects and autoregressive reasoning traces. Let $Q$ denote a prompt, $V\in\{0,1\}$ a judged verbalization of evaluation awareness, and $R\in\{0,1\}$ refusal of a harmful request. The source defines $V$ behaviorally: spontaneous text expressing awareness of testing. It deliberately does not assume a particular hidden belief or mechanism.

Original teaching case: two different associations

The pooled difference $E[R\mid V=1]-E[R\mid V=0]$ can reflect both within-prompt variation and differences in which prompts produce verbalizations. Consider an invented population with equally many easy-to-refuse and hard-to-refuse prompts. Suppose easy prompts have refusal probability $0.9$ and verbalization probability $0.8$, whereas hard prompts have refusal probability $0.1$ and verbalization probability $0.2$. Within each prompt class, assume refusal and verbalization are independent.

Bayes' rule gives $P(\text{easy}\mid V=1)=0.8$ and $P(\text{easy}\mid V=0)=0.2$. Thus

$$E[R\mid V=1]=0.8(0.9)+0.2(0.1)=0.74,$$ $$E[R\mid V=0]=0.2(0.9)+0.8(0.1)=0.26.$$

The pooled association is 48 percentage points despite zero within-class effect by construction. This is why the source's within-question analysis is consequential. Even within a fixed question, however, stochastic reasoning can jointly cause both $V$ and $R$. Conditioning on $Q$ removes one confound, not every confound.

An intervention asks a different question: compare refusal probabilities when a selected reasoning sentence is inserted, retained, or removed under a specified continuation protocol. This estimates the causal effect of that text intervention. It is not automatically the effect of an abstract variable called awareness, because the sentence also contains semantic information about safety and user intent.

Survey, intervention, and rewriting

Section 1 reports manually verified verbalizations across all eight models and nineteen benchmarks tested, with 515 verified instances. Open-weight traces and closed-model summaries provide different observation channels, limiting direct prevalence comparisons. Section 2 reports within-question associations with safer behavior, while also showing that prompts with any observed verbalization differ markedly from prompts with none. “At least one in 500” is a sampling-dependent grouping, not a sharp biological-style class of aware prompts.

Section 3 provides stronger causal evidence for Kimi K2.5 on Fortress. The authors resample before and after selected sentences and inject different sentences. Removing awareness-related sentences increases compliance; more assertive and specific injected sentences increase refusal. Their helpfulness reframings weaken the effect. These controls indicate that sentence content matters. The experiment does not establish the same causal effect across every surveyed model or benchmark.

The resampling procedure rejects replacement continuations that again verbalize awareness. That defines a selected intervention distribution, rather than an unrestricted natural continuation after deletion. A rigorous interpretation names this condition. It also distinguishes relative percentage changes from percentage-point changes when reading figures and summaries.

Section 4 analyzes recurring cues and rewrites prompts. Reduced awareness and refusal can reflect improved realism, but rewriting may change intent, clarity, or difficulty. Semantic equivalence needs independent adjudication. The iterative procedure optimizes a monitor outcome, so fresh held-out prompts and evaluators matter. A low awareness score by itself cannot certify ecological validity.

Section 5 exposes a subtle failure: steering may reduce explicit testing language by inserting another user persona. “Research,” “harmful use,” and casual-conversation directions can lower awareness while changing refusal differently. Evaluation realism requires preserving the target intent, not merely suppressing the model's commentary about evaluation.

Exercises

  1. Why does deleting an awareness sentence not uniquely intervene on awareness?
  2. If a prompt verbalizes awareness independently with probability $0.01$ per rollout, find the probability of at least one verbalization in 100 and 500 rollouts.
  3. Design a control for a rewrite that reduces both awareness and refusal.
