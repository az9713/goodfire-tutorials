Why can a one-dimensional concept need several coordinates?

A neural representation can have more linear directions than intrinsic degrees of freedom. If a model represents an angle by its cosine and sine, two activation coordinates jointly encode one periodic variable. Recovering either coordinate separately misses the topology of the represented quantity.

Let $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$ be an angle and let $u(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^T$. Its image is the unit circle. Locally, one real coordinate specifies position on the curve, so its intrinsic dimension is one. Its linear span is all of $\mathbb{R}^2$, because $u(0)$ and $u(\pi/2)$ are linearly independent. Intrinsic dimension and dimension of the linear span answer different questions.

A straight intervention can leave a curved representation

Compare the midpoint between $u(0)=(1,0)$ and $u(\pi/2)=(0,1)$ with the point halfway along the shorter circular arc. The chord midpoint is $(1/2,1/2)$ and has norm $1/\sqrt2$. The arc midpoint is $u(\pi/4)=(1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2)$ and has norm one.

Only the second point stays on the stipulated representation manifold. If a network has only learned behavior near the unit circle, its response to the chord midpoint need not correspond to a meaningful intermediate angle. This construction motivates manifold-aware steering, but does not prove that every off-manifold intervention fails or that every fitted manifold is the model's true ontology.

Steering Along Manifolds and The World Inside Neural Networks ask how to identify geometry that predicts useful control. Their empirical validation must be read separately from this elementary circle calculation.

Addition as a rotation

For integers modulo a positive integer $M$, define $\omega=2\pi/M$ and $u_M(a)=(\cos(\omega a),\sin(\omega a))^T$. The angle-addition identities imply

$$u_M(a+b)=R(\omega a)u_M(b),\qquad R(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos t&-\sin t\\\sin t&\cos t\end{pmatrix}.$$

The first coordinate of the matrix product is $\cos(\omega a)\cos(\omega b)-\sin(\omega a)\sin(\omega b)=\cos(\omega(a+b))$. The second is $\sin(\omega(a+b))$. Adding a multiple of $M$ changes the angle by a multiple of $2\pi$, leaving both coordinates unchanged.

For $M=12$, $a=2$, and $b=3$, rotating $u_{12}(3)=(0,1)$ by $\pi/3$ yields $(-\sqrt3/2,1/2)=u_{12}(5)$. This is a concrete mechanism that could implement cyclic addition. Establishing that an actual language model uses such a mechanism requires tracing and intervening on its computations, as discussed in A Geometric Calculator Inside a Neural Network.

Sparse coding adds an optimization problem

Let $h\in\mathbb{R}^d$ be an activation vector, $D\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}$ a dictionary, and $z\in\mathbb{R}^m$ code coefficients. A common sparse-coding objective is

$$\min_{D,z}\ \frac12\|h-Dz\|_2^2+\lambda\|z\|_1,$$

where $\lambda\gt0$ trades reconstruction error against coefficient magnitude. A sparse autoencoder, or SAE, learns an encoder that predicts codes from activations, alongside a decoder dictionary. Practical variants use different sparsity mechanisms and constraints.

The displayed objective needs a scale convention. Without constraining dictionary scale, reconstruction and sparsity can be traded artificially.

Replace $D$ by $cD$ and $z$ by $z/c$ for $c\gt0$. Their product is unchanged, while the penalty becomes $\lambda\|z\|_1/c$. Letting $c$ grow drives this penalty toward zero without changing the represented activation. A normalization constraint or another mechanism fixing scale is therefore necessary for the intended tradeoff.

A small reconstruction error does not prove that the recovered coefficients correspond to the original network's causal variables. Dictionaries can have permutations, correlated atoms, feature splitting, or other alternative decompositions. The Goodfire papers on SAEs and neural geometry, SAE scaling, and block-sparse featurizers examine different parts of this problem.

Exercises

  1. Compute the norm of the midpoint of two unit-circle points separated by angle $\Delta\in[0,\pi]$. When is the chord midpoint the origin?
  2. For $M=7$, give the representation of $a+b$ when $a=5$ and $b=4$. Explain which algebraic identity justifies wrapping around.
  3. A two-dimensional point cloud forms a circle after PCA. List two checks needed before treating it as a causal concept manifold rather than a visualization artifact.
